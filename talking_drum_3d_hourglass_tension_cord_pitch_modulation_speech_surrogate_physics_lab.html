1. Dynamic Cord-Tension Modulation $T(t)$

Unlike fixed-tension drums, the talking drum mounts two opposing animal-hide membranes (goat, kid, or iguana skin) across a waist-constricted hourglass shell linked by longitudinal leather tension cords (osán). Squeezing the cords between the upper arm and ribs increases radial tension $T(t) = T_0(1 + \kappa_{\text{cord}} S(t))$, continuously shifting the Bessel modal frequencies $f_{mn}(t) = \frac{j_{mn}}{2\pi a}\sqrt{T(t)/\sigma}$ across more than an octave—both between strokes and mid-note after impact.

2. Hourglass Venturi Throat & Vocal Formants

The narrow hourglass waist constricts the internal air column between the struck batter head and the sympathetic bottom head. Acting as an acoustic Venturi neck joining two resonant sub-cavities, the throat introduces a vocal formant doublet ($290\text{–}920\text{ Hz}$) that colors every pitch glide with human vowel-like resonances (/o/, /a/, /e/).

3. Yoruba 3-Tone Surrogacy (Dò – Rè – Mí)

Tone languages such as Yoruba distinguish words by three level pitch registers—Low (Dò), Mid (Rè), and High (Mí)—plus rising and falling glides. By replicating exact speech-tone contours and syllable durations, master drummers transmit proverbs, royal Oríkì praise poetry, and long-distance messages (historically 4–5 miles per drum, using Carrington's redundant poetic formulas to eliminate ambiguity).

4. Eastern vs. Western Playing Styles & Saworo

In the Eastern zone (Nigeria, Benin, Ghana — Yoruba Dùndún/Gángan, Dagomba Lunna), players strike with a curved wooden stick (ọ̀pá) for long, vocal pitch-bent notes accompanied by jingling peripheral brass bells (saworo). In the Western zone (Senegal/Gambia/Mali — Wolof Tama), players combine rapid stick strokes with explosive free-hand finger rolls for Mbalax rhythms.
